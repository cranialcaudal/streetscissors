## The fast

The Carmelite fast comes from the Rule. The hermits living on Mount Carmel asked Saint Albert, the Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem, for a way of life, and he wrote one for them between 1206 and 1214. Pope Innocent IV confirmed it, with some changes, in 1247. Its sixteenth chapter reads:

> *Ieiunium singulis diebus, exceptis Dominicis, observetis a festo Exaltationis Sanctae Crucis usque ad diem Dominicae Resurrectionis, nisi infirmitas vel debilitas corporis, aut alia iusta causa, ieiunium solvi suadeat, quia necessitas non habet legem.*
>
> Keep the fast every day except Sundays, from the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross until the day of the Lord's Resurrection, unless sickness or weakness of body, or some other just cause, counsels that the fast be broken, for necessity has no law. (Rule of Saint Albert, 16; translated here from the Latin)

The chapter has three parts: a season, from September 14 to Easter; an exception, every Sunday; and a release, for anyone whose health or circumstances call for one. The next chapter adds abstinence from meat, with the same release for the sick. The panel at the top of this page says each day whether the season is running.

## Why fasting is strong

Fasting is not strong by itself. Kept for its own sake it is a diet, and kept to be seen it has, in the Lord's words, already had its reward ([Matthew 6:16-18](/faith/bible/matthew/6#v16)). Its strength comes from what it is joined to.

Saint Thomas Aquinas names three purposes (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 147, a. 1):

> First, in order to bridle the lusts of the flesh [...]. Secondly, we have recourse to fasting in order that the mind may arise more freely to the contemplation of heavenly things: hence it is related of Daniel that he received a revelation from God after fasting for three weeks. Thirdly, in order to satisfy for sins.

The second purpose is the one closest to Carmel. The chapter of the Rule often called its heart tells each hermit to remain in his cell "meditating day and night on the law of the Lord and watching in prayer" (Rule, 10), and the fast serves that. Hunger holds the attention; a fast withholds the meal and leaves the attention free for prayer. Scripture seldom speaks of fasting alone:

- The Lord fasts forty days, and answers the tempter that man does not live by bread alone ([Matthew 4:1-4](/faith/bible/matthew/4#v1)).
- Of one unclean spirit he says, "This kind is able to be expelled by nothing other than prayer and fasting" ([Mark 9:28](/faith/bible/mark/9#v28)).
- "Prayer with fasting is good, and almsgiving is better than hiding away gold in storage" ([Tobit 12:8](/faith/bible/tobit/12#v8)).
- The fast God has chosen is to "relieve the burdens that oppress" and to "break your bread with the hungry" ([Isaiah 58:6-7](/faith/bible/isaiah/58#v6)).
- "Be converted to me with your whole heart, in fasting and weeping and mourning" ([Joel 2:12](/faith/bible/joel/2#v12)).

Pope Leo XIV, in his message for Lent 2026, *Listening and Fasting*, writes: "Precisely because it involves the body, fasting makes it easier to recognize what we 'hunger' for and what we deem necessary for our sustenance."

Fasting, prayer and almsgiving belong together, as the Lord teaches them together in the sixth chapter of Matthew.

## Its measure

The Church's law asks less than the Rule does.

- **Days.** Fasting and abstinence from meat on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday. Abstinence on Fridays, unless a solemnity falls on one (canon 1251); in the United States the bishops require it on the Fridays of Lent and, on other Fridays, commend it while allowing another penance in its place.
- **Ages.** Abstinence binds from the fourteenth birthday. Fasting binds from the eighteenth birthday until the fifty-ninth, the beginning of the sixtieth year (canon 1252).
- **Amount.** A fast day allows one full meal. Some food may be taken at two other times, which in the United States is reckoned as two smaller meals that together do not equal the full one, with no solid food between meals.

The fast of the Rule is longer than this, and it is not a law for the laity. A lay person who keeps it does so as a devotion, and under the Rule's own closing clause. Saint Thomas says the mean of this virtue "is measured not according to quantity but according to right reason" (II-II, q. 147, a. 1, ad 2). A fast that leaves a person unable to work, to be patient or to pray is no longer reasonable. Those who are ill, pregnant or nursing are excused even from the Church's law, and anyone with a medical condition or a history of disordered eating should ask a doctor first. A confessor or spiritual director is the proper judge of a doubtful case.

## The day it belongs to

The fast is kept within a day of prayer, and these pages hold that day:

- **Morning.** [Morning Prayer](/faith/hours/lauds), then a time of mental prayer, which Saint Teresa of Jesus describes as friendly conversation, often and alone, with the One who we know loves us (*Life*, 8).
- **Midday.** The [Angelus](/faith/angelus), and the [readings of the Mass](/faith/readings).
- **Evening.** [Evening Prayer](/faith/hours/vespers) and the [Rosary](/faith/rosary).
- **Night.** [Night Prayer](/faith/hours/compline), with an examination of conscience.

The Rule keeps silence from after Night Prayer until after the first prayer of the next morning, and supports it from the prophet: "In silence and hope will be your strength" (Rule, 21, quoting [Isaiah 30:15](/faith/bible/isaiah/30#v15)). Pope Leo's message extends fasting to speech in the same way, asking for a fast that "extends to our use of language, so that hurtful words may diminish and give way to a greater space for the voice of others."

## The calendar

The fast follows the liturgical year. Sunday is never a fast day. Friday is a day of penance throughout the year (canon 1250). The season of the Rule opens on the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, September 14, and ends with Holy Saturday. These pages also count a solemnity as free of the fast; the Rule names only Sundays, but the Church keeps no penance on a solemnity that falls on a Friday, and the same reasoning is applied here.

The days are reckoned by the calendar of the Discalced Carmelites, so the Order's own celebrations appear: Our Lady of Mount Carmel on July 16, Saint Teresa of Jesus on October 15, Saint John of the Cross on December 14.

## The poor

Isaiah's fast ends in bread shared, and Lent has always joined fasting to almsgiving. The traditional practice is to give to the poor what the fast has saved.

## In secret

"But as for you, when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, so that your fasting will not be apparent to men, but to your Father, who is in secret" ([Matthew 6:17-18](/faith/bible/matthew/6#v17)). These pages record nothing about the reader and keep no count of days.

## About these pages

**The Bible** is the Catholic Public Domain Version, a translation of the Latin Vulgate by Ronald L. Conte Jr., with all seventy-three books; [it can be read whole](/faith/bible). It is one translator's work and carries no imprimatur. Its psalms have the Vulgate's numbers, which from Psalm 10 to Psalm 147 are one lower than the Hebrew numbers most modern Bibles print, so the psalm widely known as the twenty-third is here the twenty-second.

**The Hours** follow the four-week psalter of the Liturgy of the Hours, so the psalms and canticles are those appointed for the day, in this translation. The approved English text, with its antiphons, intercessions and concluding prayers, is under copyright and is not reproduced, and the proper offices of feasts are not included. This is not the official text, and it does not fulfil an obligation to pray the Office.

**The readings at Mass** are those of the lectionary used in the dioceses of the United States. The passages are the appointed ones; the wording is this Bible's, not the translation read in church. Readings proper to the Carmelite Order are not included.

**The saints' lives** are the opening summaries of their English Wikipedia articles, shown with their source.

**The prayers** are given in their traditional English wording.
